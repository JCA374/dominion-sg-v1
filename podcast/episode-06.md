# Episode 06: The race, and an honest no

This is the episode with the answer. Two training branches started from our best network. Branch A got plain reinforcement training. Branch B first learned the teacher's verified lessons, and then got exactly the same training. Same settings, same three hundred thousand games per run, the same kingdoms and opponents at every step. Two random seeds each. The question was simple. Does B end up stronger than A?

First, the mathematics of the final test, because it decides how to read the numbers. Each run was first tested on five hundred fresh kingdoms, both seats, against our frozen best network, an earlier best network, an older champion, and the genetic algorithm player. From those, the stronger seed of each branch went to the promotion match. That match is two thousand kingdoms that no model has ever seen, both seats, four thousand games, played once. The gate was written before any of this: at least fifty three percent against the frozen network, with the lower end of the ninety five percent interval above fifty.

Why so strict? Remember the winner's curse from episode two. If you choose the best of several noisy results, its score is flattered. The promotion match is the cure, because it is fresh and played only once. And this time the curse showed up exactly as the mathematics predicts.

Here are the results. In the five hundred kingdom test, branch B with the second seed looked best of all. It scored fifty four point six percent against the frozen network, with an interval from about fifty two to fifty seven. That is why it was chosen. On the two thousand fresh kingdoms, it scored forty nine point nine percent. The interval runs from forty eight point six to fifty one point two. The lead was luck. Branch A's chosen run scored fifty one point four, from fifty point zero to fifty two point eight. Neither reaches the gate. Nothing is promoted.

Head to head, on the same seed, B beat A fifty three point eight percent with the first seed, an interval from fifty one point two to fifty six and a half. With the second seed, fifty one point two, and that interval includes fifty. So one seed says B is a little better, and the other says we cannot tell. That is not the consistent improvement the plan required. Against the genetic algorithm player, both branches held their strength, about eighty six to eighty seven percent, as the frozen network does. No card made either branch weaker.

Then I asked where B and A actually differ. I recorded two hundred games of B against A. They disagree on sixteen percent of decisions, far more than the seven percent of buys the supervised step changed. So the hundred iterations of training moved both branches around much more than our nudge did. And the disagreements sit where B is nearly indifferent between its top two options. On the held out bank, with exact values, B's differing buys were worth about a quarter of a point of win chance on average. Better in nineteen percent of cases, worse in sixteen. A wash.

What did we learn? The teacher is real. Searching to the end of the game beats our network by six to nine points. But a one time nudge of about three hundred lessons did not survive further training. Each lesson is a rare exception, and three hundred of them do not form a rule the network can generalise. The plan said to extend the supervised approach only if it consistently beat plain training. It did not, so I stopped there.

If someone tries again, two changes look promising. Many more verified lessons, tens of thousands, since twenty thousand labels gave three hundred and eighty nine. Or a signal that continues during training, so search keeps correcting the network at every iteration instead of once at the start.

Along the way, the training code got real fixes: the temperature bug, rejected broken games, reproducible seeds, and tests for all of it. Those stay. This was an honest no, and it is written down with all its numbers.
